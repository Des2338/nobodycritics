Resident Evil (2026)

*A Great-Looking Horror Movie With a Story That Doesn’t Add Up*

NOBODY CRITICS | DANIEL’S REVIEW

Rating: 6/10 • Director: Zach Cregger • Starring: Austin Abrams as Bryan Hodukavich

Spoilers: YES — Major plot details discussed

Tags: Horror, Action, Science Fiction, Survival Horror, Body Horror, Dark Comedy, Resident Evil, Zombie, Monster

Rating: 6 / 10

Horror Action Science Fiction Survival Horror Body Horror Dark Comedy Resident Evil Zombie Monster

Looks Great. Story? Not So Much.

Visually, this is probably one of the better Resident Evil movies we've gotten. The effects, colors, and camerawork are impressive, and a few sequences genuinely had me hooked. But I keep hearing that it's the best Resident Evil movie ever made, and story-wise, I just can't agree. My biggest issue is Bryan, the main character, and why he keeps doing what he does.

An Average Guy Still Needs a Reason

The defense I keep hearing is that Bryan isn't a hero. He's just an average guy thrown into a nightmare. Fine. I like that setup. But being ordinary isn't character development, and looking scared isn't the same as making believable decisions.

Bryan's girlfriend is pregnant, and he's unsure about becoming a father. Then he sees a woman who clearly isn't human kill a cop. After witnessing that, he stumbles into an abandoned farmhouse to try to contact his girlfriend. That's who matters to him. He's not stopping to call somebody and say he's still on schedule with the package. He's trying to reach someone he loves because something is seriously wrong. At the farmhouse, even the dog turns out to be infected—its head splits open and these tentacle-like things come shooting out. And somehow, he's still committed to that delivery. Dude, what?

He's offered double pay. That makes sense for a miserable delivery, not after you've watched a woman who shouldn't be alive kill somebody. At that point, I'd be saying, 'Fuck the package. I need to get home.' His concern for his girlfriend is established, but his actions don't follow it. He keeps going because the plot needs him to.

The film treats him like an average guy, but repeatedly risking his life to finish a dangerous mission is a hero arc. That's not the problem. The problem is that the movie doesn't earn it.

The Cure Reveal Comes Too Late

The movie eventually tells Bryan that he's delivering the cure—but not until roughly halfway through. That's the information he needed at the beginning. If the nurse had told him right away that the package could save lives, then his decision to keep going would have some weight. Add the money he could earn for his possible child, and now there's an actual reason for him to take that risk.

Until he knows what's inside, though, getting back to his girlfriend makes a hell of a lot more sense than protecting a mystery package. Learning the truth later doesn't explain why he kept choosing the delivery earlier.

Austin Abrams Is Doing His Job

I want to separate the writing from the acting. Austin Abrams is good. I liked him in Weapons, and he's also appeared in Scary Stories to Tell in the Dark, The Walking Dead, and Euphoria. From what I've seen, he does a fantastic job with the characters he's given, and he sells Bryan's fear here. My issue isn't with his performance. It's with what the script asks his character to do.

There is one thing about Bryan's reactions that wears on me, though. He hesitates so often that it starts to feel like every monster is the first one he's seen. I don't need him to become some zombie-killing badass. I just want to see a little more adaptation after everything he's survived. That feels more like a choice in how the character is written and directed than a failure on Abrams' part.

The Horror Looks Fantastic. The Comedy? Not So Much.

This is where the movie really delivers. The maternity ward sequence is nasty in the best way, with effects that make you want to stare and look away at the same time. The infected dog's head splitting open is another standout. It has that gross, unpredictable body-horror feel I love. The colors have life to them, and the camera doesn't fight you when the action gets chaotic.

My favorite shot is probably Bryan running through the city as infected people drop off buildings and crash onto cars. There's so much happening in that scene, but you can follow it. The camera moves with the action instead of turning everything into a shaky mess. It pulls you right into the chaos. That is the kind of filmmaking I wanted more of.

It's a shame the dark comedy didn't work nearly as well for me, because Zach Cregger has already shown he can direct it. He directed Barbarian, with Justin Long, and I thought that was a fantastic movie. Here, some of the jokes come from characters standing around hesitating and arguing when they should be moving. There's a scene where a woman gets out of a vehicle, ends up in danger, and the others drag out this back-and-forth. I'm sitting there thinking, 'She's dead. We need to get the fuck out of here.' Instead of cutting the tension in a funny way, the banter just slows everything down.

I Wish We Had Stayed in the Snow

Those early snowy-woods scenes had the makings of a better survival movie. Bryan's stranded, finding bodies and creatures he can't explain, and trying to reach his girlfriend. I'd have loved to follow him through the woods, along isolated roads, and maybe into a small town as he tries to get home. Once we move into Raccoon City, the spectacle gets bigger, but for me the story loses that tight, scary feeling.

What if he ditched the package, fought to get home, and only at the end did we learn it held the cure? He chose his family without knowing he might have saved everyone. Holy shit, that's a dark ending. Or what if he finally got home, transformed, and killed the person he'd been trying to protect? Either route would have made the pregnancy storyline hit harder.

And that dog scene sent me down another rabbit hole. I saw more of John Carpenter's The Thing in that transformation than I did Resident Evil. Imagine if the organism from The Thing actually made it out of Antarctica. Now it's reached a small, isolated town cut off from the rest of the world, and this stranger gets stranded there. Who can he trust? How does he know who's human? He doesn't know anybody, and anybody could be the creature. Same paranoia as The Thing, but with a whole town full of people and more questions. I'll stop there before I start writing a completely different movie, but the idea is there. That's how much potential I saw in those early scenes.

Does It Even Need the Resident Evil Name?

Sure, there are infected creatures, Umbrella, Raccoon City, and little hints of the games. But honestly, I'm not even sure it looks the part so much as it gives us a taste of it. The references feel thin, and the story doesn't make the most of the universe it's borrowing.

Here's another possibility: don't call it Resident Evil at all. Give it its own title and let us follow this random guy through a nightmare. Then start dropping those small Umbrella clues until the audience realizes, 'Oh my God, this is happening during the early Resident Evil outbreak.' That could have been a fun reveal. Instead, we know the franchise going in, and I kept waiting for the story to do more with it. Some of the older movies were cheesy as hell, but their characters' goals made more sense to me within the worlds those movies built.

Final Thoughts — 6/10

There's a lot I enjoyed looking at here: the practical-looking creature effects, the maternity ward, that wild city sequence, and the way the camera follows the action. Austin Abrams is strong, too. But the package motivation never clicked for me, especially after Bryan had already shown how worried he was about his pregnant girlfriend.

I wanted the story to either give him a real reason to carry that cure from the start or let him act like the ordinary guy the movie keeps telling me he is. Instead, we get a visually impressive horror movie with a character journey I never quite bought. For me, that's a 6/10.
